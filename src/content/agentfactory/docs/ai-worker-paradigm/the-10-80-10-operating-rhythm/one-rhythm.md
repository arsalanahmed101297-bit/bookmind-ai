**In everyday life.** You hire a painter. You choose the colors and say
which rooms, and you agree that you will walk through the rooms before
paying. Then you go to work, and the painter paints the rooms. When you
come home, you check the edges and the trim around the doors and windows,
and only then pay.

**At work.** Dave gives the AP Worker one task: reconcile a vendor's statement, and
change nothing. He should let it work, then read what comes back before
he closes September's accounts.

### What are the three parts?

The book's operating rule has three parts: *Humans define intent. AI
Workers execute. Humans verify outcomes.* With the painter, you define the
intent, the painter executes, and you verify the outcome. The **10-80-10**
rhythm is that rule with numbers added. The human defines the first 10
percent, the worker executes the middle 80, and the human verifies the
final 10.

The pattern is older than AI: Dan Martell's 10-80-10 rule for delegating
to people.[^martell] With AI Workers in the place of the team, it works
for AI too.[^v1-thesis] What AI adds: the worker must bring back evidence
you can check, flag what is unusual, and wait for a person before any
risky action.

Figure 3.1 shows the three parts, and the next three pages explain each.

![The 10-80-10 operating rhythm, under the rule: humans define intent,
AI Workers execute, humans verify outcomes. A bar split 10, 80 and 10
percent is a guide to who leads, not a fixed split of time. In the first
10 percent, the human leads, to Define. It sets the intent, the scope,
the authority and the review contract (checks, evidence and stop
conditions). For a standing worker, you keep up the Role Contract and
standing instructions. It delivers a brief with clear limits. In the
middle 80 percent, the AI Worker leads, to Execute. It plans and does
the work, uses tools within its authority, checks results and gathers
evidence. It stops and escalates when a rule says so. It delivers the
output, the evidence and the flagged exceptions. In the final 10
percent, the human leads, to Verify. Read the flags first, clear the
approval queue, and check a sample, more where the risk is higher. Then
correct the underlying cause. For a standing worker, review at set
times. It delivers a decision to approve or revise. A dashed arrow runs

reads: "Humans set goals, limits and approvals. AI Workers execute
within them."](img/rhythm.png)

*Figure 3.1. The 10-80-10 rhythm. You own both ends. The worker owns the middle.*

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A shape, not a timesheet**
>
> Read the numbers as a shape, not a timesheet. They describe who leads
> each part of the work, not minutes on a clock. The first time you
> delegate an unfamiliar task, both ends take longer: you write the brief
> with care and check the result line by line. A task that has run
> without errors every week for months may need five minutes of setup and
> a quick look at its evidence. **The person responsible for each part stays
> the same. Only the time each part takes changes.**

### When the worker never stops

A Standing AI Worker holds a role that never finishes. A schedule starts
the AP Worker's register run every Monday, and each vendor email starts
a run of its own, day or night. So where do your two ends go, when a run
starts at 3 a.m. and you are asleep?

**In everyday life.** Think of a house cleaner who comes every Tuesday
while you are at work. You do not stand at the door each week explaining
the job. You wrote it down once: clean these rooms, water the plants,
never throw away papers from the desk. If something is unusual (a
broken glass, a leak under the sink), the cleaner leaves you a note. If
something needs your decision ("should I throw out these old boxes?"),
the cleaner asks and waits. And now and then, you look closely at one
room to make sure the work is still good.

That is the whole answer. The rhythm still applies to every piece of
work, but you no longer do your two parts at the same time as the work.
[0.3](../../how-ai-works/ai-fluency/the-four-ds.md) showed the same move
for the 4Ds:

- **Your first 10 percent is done once, in advance.** For the cleaner,
  it is the written instructions. For the AP Worker, it is the Role
  Contract and its standing instructions. They are the setup for every
  run, until the work or the rules change.
- **Your final 10 percent happens at times you choose,** in three ways.
  An **approval queue** holds anything the worker may prepare but not
  do on its own, like the cleaner's question about the boxes. **Flags**
  mark anything unusual, like the note about the leak, and you read them
  at a set time. And **spot checks** look closely at a sample of
  ordinary work, like the one room you inspect.

For a worker, that set time goes on the **Owner** line of its
[Role Contract](../what-is-an-ai-worker/role-contract.md): who reads the flags, and when.

*Figure 3.2. The rhythm at two speeds. The worker runs whenever work arrives; you review at times you choose.*

**Each review also improves the next setup.** Suppose Thursday's flag
shows that the standing instructions never said what to do with
statements that arrive in a different currency. Dave adds that case.
From next week, every run starts from a better brief. This is the dashed
arrow in Figures 3.1 and 3.2, from the final 10 percent back to the
first: the end of one round of work becomes the start of the next.

### Why the ends belong to a person

**The two ends belong to a person, because judgment, values and
accountability cannot be handed over.** (Accountability means the work
goes out under your name.) The middle belongs to the worker, because that
is where execution scales (the work can grow without taking more of your
time).
