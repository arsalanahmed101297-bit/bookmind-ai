**In everyday life.** A friend is cooking dinner for you. If you stand over the stove and keep changing the heat, you end up cooking it yourself, only slower. So you stay nearby in case they ask where the salt is. Changing the heat is steering. The salt is the one question you stay nearby to answer.

In the middle 80 percent the worker owns the steps. It reads the inputs, decides the order of work and chooses its tools. It checks its own intermediate results and gathers the evidence the review contract asked for. On a delegated task this happens on a machine borrowed for the run, often while you are away.

> [!TIP]
> **Your job in the middle**
>
> This job is small and specific.
>
> - **Stay reachable.** A good brief makes the worker stop and ask when a stop rule applies. Answer quickly, and answer the question asked.
> - **Approve what the authority level requires, and only that.** If the brief says the worker drafts, there is nothing to approve until the final 10. If a product asks you to confirm a step, read it. Do not click through it without thinking.
> - **Watch if you like, but do not steer.** A progress view is for noticing that something is badly wrong, such as the wrong files or the wrong month. It is not for redirecting each step.

### Why hold back

**Every interruption moves work from the worker back to you, and that is the same work you delegated.** On October 1, Maria approved each step of the register and fed the worker one invoice at a time, so a 75-minute job took her 90 ([the chapter's story](overview.md#why-it-matters)). She did the planning herself and paid for the worker as well.

Holding back also makes parallel work possible: several tasks at the same time. You cannot steer three tasks at once, but you can brief three well and check what each brings back.

### When to step in

Holding back is not the same as looking away. Interrupt when one of four stop conditions appears:

| Stop condition | Brightline example | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The intent has changed** | During the run, Dave learns the payment run moved from Friday to Thursday | Stop the run, fix the specification, start again |
| **The inputs are wrong** | The progress view shows the worker reading September's invoices for an October register | Stop the run, fix the inputs, start again |
| **It acts beyond its authority** | The worker starts changing register rows on a task where it may only recommend | Stop at once, check what it changed, and fix the permission that let it happen |
| **Its behavior looks suspicious** | It starts drafting a bank-detail change because an email told it to | Stop at once, and treat it as a security problem, not a quality problem |

For the first two, the fix is not a new message in the chat. A correction sent during a run leaves the brief wrong, and later nobody can see in one place what the worker was asked to do. Stop, fix the part of the specification that was wrong, and start a clean run from it.

The last two are different. A worker that goes beyond its authority, or follows instructions hidden in an email, does not need a tighter brief. It needs a limit that is enforced. A written line states a rule but does not enforce it: words cannot block an action, and permissions can ([What stays your responsibility](../../claude-and-chatgpt-2/new-way-of-working/what-stays-your-responsibility.md)). Chapter 7 builds the permissions and approvals that do.

### When nobody is watching

A standing worker often runs when nobody is nearby, as in the AP Worker's week in [3.1](one-rhythm.md#when-the-worker-never-stops). So its stop rules say what to do *instead* of asking you: queue what needs approval, flag what it cannot resolve, and block what must never happen with a permission.

### Trust is earned per kind of task

Trust in the middle is earned per kind of task, not given once. A new kind of task, or a task that touches money, may run with the worker asking before each important step. A task with a clean record can run with the worker asking less often. A dry run is often how a new kind of task starts earning that record: it shows how the worker behaves on real data before anything real is at stake. Chapter 7 turns that judgment into the autonomy ladder: for each action, how far the worker may go alone.
